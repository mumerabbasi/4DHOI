\chapter{Related Work}
\label{chap:related-work}

\chapterabstract{This chapter positions the thesis within prior work on human-scene interaction synthesis. It organizes the literature along two axes that are central to the thesis: trained versus zero-shot methods, and static 3D versus dynamic 4D interaction synthesis.}

\section{Foundations for Metric Human-Scene Interaction}
\label{sec:foundations-metric-hsi}

Human-scene interaction synthesis requires reasoning jointly about the geometry of the environment, the articulation of the human body, and the physical contacts that make an interaction plausible. Unlike image-only human generation, where the output only needs to appear visually consistent from a particular viewpoint, 3D human-scene interaction requires the synthesized body to be metrically compatible with the surrounding scene. A body that looks plausible in a rendered image may still float above the floor, penetrate furniture, miss the intended contact surface, or touch an object part that is semantically irrelevant to the requested interaction. For this reason, the foundations of this thesis are metric scene reconstruction, parametric human body modeling, and explicit contact-aware optimization.

The scene side of the problem is represented by a reconstructed indoor environment together with calibrated image observations. Datasets such as ScanNet and ScanNet++ provide RGB-D views, camera parameters, and reconstructed 3D geometry, enabling image-level evidence to be related back to a common scene coordinate frame \citep{dai2017scannet,yeshwanth2023scannetpp}. This calibration is essential for the setting considered in this thesis. The method starts from a scene image and its camera parameters, but the desired output is not merely a 2D depiction of a person. Instead, the goal is to synthesize a 3D body that is placed in the original reconstructed scene. The availability of the camera intrinsics, extrinsics, and scene mesh makes it possible to project image-space cues into 3D, reason about body-scene distances, and enforce geometric constraints during optimization.

On the human side, this thesis uses SMPL-X as the body representation \citep{pavlakos2019smplx}. SMPL-X represents the human body as a differentiable parametric mesh with articulated body pose, hand pose, facial expression, shape, and global transformation parameters. Its differentiability makes it suitable for optimization-based refinement, while its dense surface provides a natural representation for contact reasoning. However, the SMPL-X mesh alone does not define which parts of the body should participate in an interaction. Physical and functional interaction reasoning requires additional semantic structure over the body surface. For example, grasping a handle primarily involves the inner hand surface, leaning may involve the back or shoulder region, sitting involves the pelvis, thighs, or hips, and stable standing requires foot-floor contact. Therefore, interaction synthesis benefits from grouping body vertices into contact-relevant regions rather than treating the body as an undifferentiated surface.

Previous work has shown that 3D human pose estimation and synthesis improve substantially when the surrounding scene is treated as an active constraint rather than as a passive background. PROX introduced scene-aware body fitting by extending monocular body estimation with contact and interpenetration constraints in reconstructed 3D scenes \citep{hassan2019prox}. Its key observation is that many visually plausible monocular body estimates are physically inconsistent when inspected in 3D: the body may intersect furniture, miss supporting surfaces, or violate basic spatial constraints. PROX addresses this by penalizing body-scene penetration and encouraging selected body regions to contact nearby scene surfaces. This formulation is important for later HSI synthesis methods because it establishes contact and exclusion as explicit optimization objectives.

Subsequent work further developed contact as a learnable and semantically meaningful representation. POSA augments the SMPL-X body with body-surface contact probabilities and semantic scene labels, learning where the body is likely to contact the environment for plausible human-scene configurations \citep{hassan2021posa}. PLACE similarly emphasizes proximity and contact between articulated humans and 3D environments \citep{zhang2020place}. These works demonstrate that realistic HSI cannot be reduced to estimating a natural human pose independently of the scene. Instead, plausible interaction depends on the compatibility between specific body regions and specific scene surfaces.

This body-region view of contact is particularly important for functional interaction. Many user instructions are not fully specified by object category alone. For example, opening a drawer requires contact with a drawer handle or front panel, not merely with the cabinet as a whole. Turning on a faucet requires reaching the control region, not simply touching the sink. Sitting, leaning, pushing, grasping, and stepping each imply different body-side contact regions and different scene-side contact surfaces. Thus, a central requirement for open-vocabulary HSI is to bridge high-level language descriptions and low-level metric contact constraints. The body must be articulated naturally, but it must also place the correct body part on the correct functional region of the scene.

Recent monocular human mesh recovery methods provide useful initialization for this optimization problem. In this thesis, GVHMR is used to obtain an initial SMPL-X body estimate from the inpainted human image \citep{shen2024gvhmr}. GVHMR recovers world-grounded human motion by predicting human pose in gravity-view coordinates, which helps reduce ambiguity in monocular reconstruction. Although such methods can provide a strong initial body configuration, they do not by themselves guarantee correct interaction with a reconstructed scene. The recovered body may still require refinement to satisfy scene contact, avoid penetration, and align with the intended interaction semantics.

The final foundation is therefore optimization in the reconstructed scene. Given an initial SMPL-X body and projected scene-side contact targets, the body can be refined using losses that encourage contact with the target regions, penalize interpenetration with the scene mesh, and preserve a plausible human pose. Pose and orientation priors are important because contact objectives alone may produce unnatural configurations if the body is allowed to move freely. In this thesis, these priors also keep the optimized body close to the GVHMR initialization while allowing sufficient adjustment for physical interaction. This combination of image-based initialization, explicit body-part contact regions, projected scene contact targets, and scene-aware SMPL-X optimization provides the metric foundation for the proposed human-scene interaction synthesis pipeline.

\section{Datasets and Supervision for HSI and HOI}
\label{sec:datasets-supervision-hsi-hoi}

Progress in human-scene interaction has been closely tied to the availability of datasets that capture scenes, bodies, objects, contacts, and language at different levels of detail. However, no single dataset provides all forms of supervision needed for open-vocabulary, fine-grained functional 3D HSI. Some datasets provide high-quality reconstructed scenes but no interacting humans; others capture humans in scenes but cover a limited number of environments and actions; object-centric datasets provide detailed human-object contacts but usually do not represent full reconstructed rooms; and language-conditioned motion datasets provide text supervision but often rely on synthetic alignments or closed action spaces. This diversity of datasets has shaped the design of existing methods and also motivates training-free approaches that use pretrained priors rather than requiring paired 3D human-scene interaction examples.

Early human-scene datasets established the importance of observing humans in metric environments. PiGraphs collected RGB-D observations of human activities and represented them as interaction graphs between body parts and scene geometry, linking human pose, object geometry, contact, and visual attention \citep{savva2016pigraphs}. PROX later introduced RGB-D sequences of people interacting with reconstructed indoor scenes and showed that monocular body estimates can be improved by enforcing 3D scene constraints \citep{hassan2019prox}. Such datasets are foundational because they make it possible to study contact, support, and collision in a shared coordinate frame. At the same time, their scale and semantic coverage are limited: the number of scenes, object categories, and interaction types is small compared with the open-ended variety of everyday human-scene interactions.

A separate line of work focuses on high-quality static or reconstructed indoor scenes without necessarily providing paired human interaction data. ScanNet and ScanNet++ are examples of this category \citep{dai2017scannet,yeshwanth2023scannetpp}. They provide RGB-D or high-fidelity RGB observations, camera calibration, and reconstructed scene geometry, making them suitable for metric scene understanding. This thesis uses ScanNet++ as its scene source: each example consists of a scene image, the corresponding camera parameters, and the reconstructed scene mesh. This choice provides accurate geometry and camera alignment, but it also means that the method cannot rely on ground-truth humans, motions, or contact annotations in the target scenes. The task is therefore not to imitate captured interactions from the dataset, but to synthesize a plausible SMPL-X body in a previously reconstructed scene.

Human-object interaction datasets provide another important form of supervision. GRAB captures whole-body grasping motions with SMPL-X bodies, object poses, and contact between the body and manipulated objects \citep{taheri2020grab}. BEHAVE provides multi-view RGB-D recordings, SMPL body fits, object fits, and annotated contacts for full-body human-object interactions \citep{bhatnagar2022behave}. InterCap further improves whole-body and object tracking from multi-view RGB-D input and includes interactions with daily objects where hand and foot contacts are important \citep{huang2024intercap}. Other datasets such as COUCH, CHAIRS, and OMOMO focus on more specific interaction regimes, including chair interactions, articulated objects, and object-motion-guided full-body manipulation \citep{zhang2022couch,jiang2023chairs,li2023omomo}. These datasets are valuable because they provide detailed evidence about how bodies contact objects. However, they are usually object-centric rather than full-scene and open-vocabulary. They do not directly solve the problem of locating the functional region of an arbitrary object in a reconstructed room from a natural language instruction.

Dynamic HSI datasets extend the supervision from isolated objects to motions in scenes. HUMANISE aligns human motions with 3D indoor scenes and adds language descriptions, enabling language-conditioned motion generation in 3D environments \citep{wang2022humanise}. GIMO provides human motion, 3D scene context, egocentric views, and gaze information, supporting intent-aware motion prediction in real environments \citep{zheng2022gimo}. EgoBody captures 3D human pose and shape during social interactions from egocentric devices in complex scenes \citep{zhang2022egobody}. TRUMANS scales dynamic HSI further by capturing motion in many indoor scene configurations with contact-aware interactions \citep{jiang2024trumans}. These datasets support training models for navigation, sitting, standing, object-directed motion, and long-horizon interaction. Their main relevance to this thesis is that they illustrate the cost of paired motion-scene supervision: collecting accurate human motion, scene geometry, object states, and contact over time is expensive and difficult to scale to open-vocabulary tasks.

The type of supervision also determines the generalization behavior of trained HSI methods. Methods trained on paired scene-motion data can learn strong priors for common actions, but they are constrained by the distribution of captured interactions. Methods trained with semantic action-object labels, such as COINS, can synthesize interactions from controlled action-object specifications, but the interaction vocabulary and object semantics remain predefined \citep{zhao2022coins}. Methods trained with contact annotations can learn where bodies usually touch objects, but contact labels are expensive to obtain and often tied to a limited set of object categories or actions. These supervision regimes are effective when the test interaction resembles the training data, but they are less suitable when the instruction refers to an unseen functional element, an unusual scene layout, or a fine-grained object part such as a drawer handle, faucet control, cabinet edge, or window latch.

This thesis uses a different form of supervision. It does not train on paired 3D human-scene interaction data from ScanNet++ or any other HSI dataset. Instead, it uses pretrained models and explicit geometric optimization. The body-side contact regions are manually defined on SMPL-X as subsets of semantically meaningful parts, including hands, arms, back, head, legs, hips, and feet. These regions are selected to correspond to surfaces that commonly participate in contact, such as the inner hand surface, downward-facing foot vertices, and backside regions of the head, back, hips, and legs. These annotations are used both to define contact losses during optimization and to evaluate whether the synthesized interaction places the appropriate body region in contact with the scene. The scene-side contact regions, however, are not provided by the dataset. They are inferred from image-space evidence and projected back to the reconstructed mesh.

The resulting research gap is therefore not simply a lack of data, but a mismatch between available supervision and the desired task. Existing datasets provide realistic scenes, captured human motions, object-level contacts, semantic labels, or language descriptions, but they rarely provide open-vocabulary task descriptions paired with fine-grained functional contact regions on reconstructed scene meshes. This motivates a training-free formulation in which pretrained visual and generative priors propose interaction evidence, while explicit projection and SMPL-X optimization enforce metric consistency in the ScanNet++ scene coordinate frame.

\section{Affordances, Functional Scene Elements, and Contact Representations}
\label{sec:affordances-functional-contact}

Human-scene interaction is governed not only by geometry but also by affordance: the possibility of action offered by a scene to a human body. In 3D HSI, affordance is usually expressed through spatial relations between the body and the environment, such as sitting on a support surface, standing on the floor, leaning against a wall, touching an object, or grasping a functional part. These relations are physical because they require metric consistency, but they are also semantic because the same object can afford different actions depending on which part is used. A chair may support sitting on its seat, touching on its backrest, or stepping near its legs; a cabinet may afford leaning, touching, opening, or grasping depending on whether the relevant region is the side surface, front panel, handle, or drawer edge. Therefore, functional HSI requires more than detecting the target object: it requires identifying which body region should contact which scene region.

Early work on interaction-centered scene understanding made this connection explicit. PiGraphs represented human activities as probabilistic interaction graphs between human body parts and scene objects, showing that everyday actions can be described through recurrent geometric and semantic relations between people and their surroundings \citep{savva2016pigraphs}. The key insight is that human pose provides evidence about scene function, and scene structure constrains plausible human behavior. This perspective remains important for later synthesis methods: a generated body should not simply be close to an object, but should instantiate a meaningful relation between an action, a body part, and a scene element.

Contact-aware body modeling further developed this idea by treating the body surface as a structured interaction representation. PROX introduced contact and interpenetration constraints for fitting SMPL-X bodies in reconstructed scenes, encouraging selected body regions to touch nearby scene surfaces while avoiding physically impossible intersections \citep{hassan2019prox}. PLACE and POSA then used contact more directly for synthesis. PLACE models proximity and contact between articulated humans and 3D environments, while POSA learns body-surface contact probabilities and scene semantics on top of SMPL-X \citep{zhang2020place,hassan2021posa}. These methods establish that plausible HSI depends on body-scene compatibility at the surface level, not only on global body pose or object proximity.

However, learned contact priors are often limited by the granularity of their supervision. A body-surface contact probability can indicate that the hand, foot, pelvis, or back is likely to contact the scene, but it does not necessarily specify the exact functional region on the target object. For many interactions, this distinction is crucial. In `sit on a chair,'' contacting the seat is more meaningful than touching a chair leg. In `turn on the faucet,'' the relevant region is the control or handle area, not the sink basin as a whole. In ``take the bike off the stand,'' the hand contact should occur on a plausible graspable part of the bike, while the feet may remain supported by the floor. Thus, object-level affordance and body-level contact are both necessary but insufficient when considered separately.

Semantic-control methods address part of this limitation by conditioning HSI generation on higher-level interaction labels. COINS synthesizes human bodies in scenes from compositional action-object specifications, such as sitting on a chair or touching a table \citep{zhao2022coins}. Its formulation is important because it recognizes interactions as semantic compositions rather than purely geometric arrangements. Nevertheless, the semantic unit remains an action-object pair. This is suitable for interactions where the object category strongly determines the contact surface, but it is less precise for functional interactions where the relevant contact is on a small or ambiguous object subregion. An instruction such as `open the bottom drawer'' or `hold the side frame of the treadmill'' requires grounding below the object-instance level.

Dense contact estimation methods study contact more directly, often from images or motion. DECO estimates dense vertex-level human-scene contact on the body surface from a single RGB image, reasoning over body parts, proximity, and scene context \citep{tripathi2023deco}. SUMMON uses ContactFormer to infer temporally consistent contact labels from human motion and then synthesize plausible scenes around those contacts \citep{ye2023summon}. These works are relevant because they show that contact can serve as an intermediate representation between human motion, scene geometry, and interaction semantics. At the same time, they mostly predict or use contact on the human side, or infer object existence from motion, rather than localizing a task-specific contact region on an existing reconstructed scene mesh.

Recent zero-shot HSI methods move closer to open-vocabulary functional reasoning. GenZI uses vision-language and image generation priors to synthesize plausible 2D human-scene interaction hypotheses from rendered views, then optimizes a 3D human body in the scene \citep{li2024genzi}. FunHSI explicitly targets open-vocabulary functional 3D HSI by identifying functional scene elements, constructing a contact graph, synthesizing a human performing the task, and refining the 3D body for physical and functional correctness \citep{liu2026funhsi}. These works demonstrate that pretrained vision-language and generative models can provide interaction knowledge beyond closed action-object datasets. They also highlight a central challenge: foundation models can propose plausible interactions, but the final 3D output still requires explicit contact grounding and geometric verification.

This thesis builds on this contact-centered view but represents interaction in a deliberately simple and task-oriented form. The intermediate Scene Interaction Graph contains nodes for human body regions and the target object, or the floor when the interaction requires foot support. Edges specify which body regions should contact the target object or floor. The body regions are manually defined on SMPL-X as contact-specific subsets of broader anatomical parts, including hands, arms, back, head, legs, hips, and feet. For example, hand contact uses the inner hand surface, foot contact uses downward-facing foot vertices, and back-facing regions are selected for body parts such as the head, back, hips, and legs. This graph does not require assigning semantic names to scene subparts such as handle, knob, or edge. Instead, the graph identifies the required body-object contact relations, and the scene-side contact evidence is later inferred visually.

The floor is treated as a special case because foot support is often spatially flexible. For interactions such as standing, walking preparation, climbing, or manipulating an object while supported by the floor, the exact foot location may vary as long as the feet contact a valid floor region and the body remains physically plausible. In contrast, object contacts such as hands on a treadmill frame, hands on a ladder, or hips on a chair require more localized scene-side evidence. This motivates separating generic support regions, such as floor contact, from target-object contacts that must be inferred from the visual and functional context of the instruction.

The main limitation of prior contact and affordance representations, from the perspective of this thesis, is therefore their mismatch with fine-grained open-vocabulary functional synthesis. Some methods learn likely human-scene contacts from paired data; others condition on object-level action labels; others estimate dense human-body contact from images or motion. Yet open-vocabulary functional HSI requires pairing the correct body contact region with the correct functional scene subregion in a specific reconstructed scene. The thesis addresses this gap by using the Scene Interaction Graph to determine the required body-side contacts, using generative visual priors to infer the corresponding scene-side contact regions, and then optimizing SMPL-X in the reconstructed scene coordinate frame.

\section{Static 3D Human-Scene Interaction Synthesis}
\label{sec:static-3d-hsi}

Static 3D human-scene interaction synthesis aims to generate a single plausible human body configuration in a 3D environment. Unlike dynamic HSI, which must model trajectories, temporal coherence, and changing contacts over time, static HSI focuses on whether one body pose is semantically appropriate and physically compatible with the scene. This problem is already challenging because the generated body must satisfy several coupled constraints: it should be anatomically plausible, placed at a meaningful location, oriented toward the relevant object or scene region, supported by nearby geometry, and free of severe interpenetration. For this reason, early static HSI methods focused on learning where humans can plausibly be placed in scenes and how body pose relates to surrounding geometry.

Putting Humans in a Scene is one of the early learning-based approaches for predicting human affordances in 3D indoor environments \citep{li2019putting}. Rather than merely detecting objects, it learns what human poses are afforded by the scene, such as sitting on a chair or standing on the floor. The method uses synthesized training data produced by combining semantic knowledge from 2D human poses with 3D geometric knowledge from indoor scenes. This work is important because it frames human placement as an affordance prediction problem: the scene is not only an obstacle field, but a structure that implies possible human configurations.

Generating 3D People in Scenes without People extends this idea by generating full SMPL-X bodies in empty 3D scenes \citep{zhang2020generating}. The method first predicts semantically plausible body configurations conditioned on the scene and then refines them using scene constraints to improve physical feasibility. Its formulation makes explicit that static HSI requires both semantic plausibility and physical consistency. A person sitting on a sofa must not only be near the sofa, but must have a pose and placement that create support contact while avoiding penetration. This two-stage pattern, in which a learned model proposes a body and a subsequent optimization or refinement step enforces geometry, remains influential in later work.

PLACE and POSA further emphasize contact-aware interaction synthesis. PLACE introduces proximity learning of articulation and contact in 3D environments, using local scene geometry to generate bodies that are compatible with surrounding surfaces \citep{zhang2020place}. POSA learns a body-centric representation of human-scene contact by predicting contact labels and scene semantics on the SMPL-X surface \citep{hassan2021posa}. These methods are significant because they move beyond global placement and treat contact as part of the representation of interaction. They show that plausible static HSI depends on local compatibility between body surfaces and scene surfaces. However, their learned priors are shaped by available interaction data and scene semantics. As a result, they are strongest for interactions that resemble the distributions observed during training.

Semantic control is introduced more explicitly in COINS, which synthesizes human-scene interactions from action-object specifications such as sitting on a chair, touching a table, or composing multiple atomic interactions \citep{zhao2022coins}. COINS represents a major step from generic scene population toward controllable HSI: the user can specify not only that a body should be placed in a scene, but also which action should be performed with which object instance. Its compositional formulation is useful because many interactions can be decomposed into simpler action-object relations. Nevertheless, the interaction is still specified at the level of predefined action and object categories. This makes the method less suited to open-vocabulary functional instructions in which the relevant contact region may be a small object part or an ambiguous functional surface rather than the object instance as a whole.

Recent diffusion-based models extend static interaction synthesis toward richer object and contact control. TriDi models 3D human-object interaction by jointly generating human, object, and interaction modalities with a three-way diffusion process \citep{petrov2025tridi}. It is relevant to static HSI because it treats contact maps and text descriptions as controllable interaction representations, rather than using body pose or object pose alone. Although TriDi is primarily object-centric rather than full-scene-centric, it reflects a broader trend: static interaction synthesis increasingly depends on intermediate contact representations that connect semantic intent to geometric constraints. At the same time, such methods remain data-driven and are typically trained on object-level interaction datasets such as GRAB and BEHAVE, which differ from open-vocabulary interaction synthesis in reconstructed indoor scenes.

Across these static synthesis methods, the field has progressed from plausible body placement, to contact-aware body-scene compatibility, to semantic and contact-conditioned interaction generation. These developments provide important foundations for this thesis. They show that static HSI is feasible when methods have access to learned priors, annotated contacts, semantic object labels, or paired interaction data. However, they also reveal limitations that are central to the proposed problem setting. Many methods depend on closed action or object vocabularies, require paired human-scene or human-object interaction data, and ground interactions at the object level rather than at the level of functional contact regions. This becomes limiting for instructions such as turning on a faucet, holding the side frame of a treadmill, climbing a ladder, or taking a bike off a stand, where the correct interaction depends on a precise contact region in the scene.

The thesis belongs to static 3D HSI as a task: it synthesizes a single SMPL-X body configuration that realizes a requested interaction in a reconstructed scene. However, it does not train a static HSI generative model from paired 3D interaction data. Instead, it uses language reasoning to determine body-part--scene relations, image generation to obtain a plausible human hypothesis, visual contact annotation to infer scene-side contact evidence, and optimization to enforce contact, collision, and pose plausibility in the reconstructed scene coordinate frame. This positions the work as a static HSI method that keeps the geometric accountability of contact-aware synthesis while avoiding dependence on closed interaction datasets.

\section{Language-Conditioned and Semantically Controlled HSI}
\label{sec:language-semantic-hsi}

Language provides a natural interface for controlling human-scene interaction synthesis. A user may specify a simple action such as `sit on the chair,'' but may also describe a more complex interaction such as `a person on the treadmill with both hands on the side frame, one foot on the treadmill belt and one foot in the air.'' Such instructions contain different types of information: the intended action, the target object, spatial relations between objects, body-part constraints, and sometimes detailed contact requirements. A language-conditioned HSI method must therefore solve more than text-to-pose generation. It must ground the instruction in the scene, identify the relevant object or region, infer the human pose or motion implied by the action, and ensure that the final body-scene relation is physically plausible.

Early semantic-control methods approached this problem through structured action-object labels rather than free-form language. COINS synthesizes compositional human-scene interactions from semantic specifications defined as action-object pairs, such as sitting on a chair or touching a table \citep{zhao2022coins}. This formulation is important because it introduces controllability into static HSI: the generated body is conditioned not only on the scene geometry but also on the intended interaction. However, the language interface is limited by a predefined set of actions and object categories. The method can compose known action-object relations, but it does not directly address open-vocabulary descriptions, fine-grained object parts, or instructions where the functional contact region must be inferred from visual context.

Language-conditioned motion generation extends semantic control to temporal HSI. HUMANISE introduced language-conditioned human motion generation in 3D scenes, connecting text descriptions with scene-aware motion sequences \citep{wang2022humanise}. It showed that text can specify both human action and spatial intent in a scene, such as walking to or interacting with a target object. However, grounding language in a 3D scene is difficult when the entire scene is encoded as a large geometric input. The model must implicitly learn which object the language refers to and how the generated body should relate to it. This motivates later methods that decompose language-conditioned HSI into more explicit grounding and generation stages.

TextSceneMotion addresses this issue by separating target-object grounding from object-centric motion generation \citep{cen2024textscenemotion}. Instead of generating motion directly from the whole scene and text, it first grounds the object described by the language and then generates motion relative to that object. This decomposition is conceptually important for this thesis because it treats language grounding as a distinct problem rather than expecting a generative model to infer all spatial correspondences implicitly. The limitation is that the grounding target is still primarily the object instance, whereas functional HSI often requires a smaller contact region on the object surface.

TeSMo also studies text-controlled scene-aware motion generation, using diffusion models to generate motions such as walking, sitting, and interacting with objects in 3D scenes \citep{yi2024tesmo}. It combines text control with start and goal constraints, allowing the user to guide both motion style and spatial intent. Move as You Say, Interact as You Can further introduces scene affordance maps as an intermediate representation between language grounding and motion generation \citep{wang2024move}. Its two-stage structure first predicts an explicit affordance map and then generates motion conditioned on that affordance representation. This is relevant because it shows that intermediate representations can help bridge the gap between free-form language and physically grounded motion. In this thesis, the intermediate representation is not an affordance map for motion generation, but a Scene Interaction Graph that specifies body-part--object or body-part--floor contact relations for static synthesis.

Open-vocabulary HSI methods use foundation models to reduce dependence on closed semantic vocabularies. GenZI generates zero-shot 3D human-scene interactions from natural language descriptions and 3D scenes by using vision-language and image inpainting models to synthesize plausible 2D interaction hypotheses, which are then lifted to a 3D body through optimization \citep{li2024genzi}. This demonstrates that pretrained 2D generative models contain useful priors about how humans interact with scenes. However, the language input mainly guides the generation of plausible human-scene compositions; the method does not explicitly focus on extracting fine-grained scene-side contact regions for specific body parts.

FunHSI is more directly aligned with functional language-conditioned interaction. It takes open-vocabulary task prompts and reasons about functional scene elements, contact graphs, and 3D body refinement \citep{liu2026funhsi}. This is an important step beyond object-category grounding because it recognizes that instructions such as increasing room temperature or opening a window require reasoning about object functionality. FunHSI shows that functional HSI benefits from explicitly representing which parts of the scene are relevant to the task and which contacts make the interaction correct. The present thesis follows the same broad motivation, but uses a different route to contact grounding: language is first converted into a Scene Interaction Graph over body parts and the target object or floor, while the precise scene-side contact region is inferred visually from generated contact annotations.

Language-conditioned HSI also appears in video generation. GenHSI decomposes controllable HSI video generation into script writing, 3D-aware keyframe generation, and animation with a pretrained video diffusion model \citep{li2026genhsi}. This pipeline is relevant because it treats language as a high-level controller that can be decomposed into atomic interaction steps before visual synthesis. It also illustrates the increasing role of foundation models in interpreting interaction descriptions and producing plausible human-scene configurations. Nevertheless, video-oriented methods emphasize temporal coherence and visual plausibility, while the static 3D setting in this thesis emphasizes metric contact, mesh alignment, and body-region-specific optimization.

The key distinction in this thesis is the granularity at which language is grounded. Prior language-conditioned HSI methods commonly map language to action labels, target objects, affordance maps, or motion trajectories. In contrast, this thesis maps language to explicit body-part--scene contact requirements. The Scene Interaction Graph specifies which human regions, such as hands, arms, back, head, hips, legs, or feet, should contact the target object or floor. The method does not require predefined semantic names for object parts such as handle, knob, edge, or seat. Instead, after language reasoning determines the body-object contact structure, visual generative models infer where those contacts should occur on the scene image, and the resulting evidence is projected to the reconstructed scene for optimization. This allows both short prompts and detailed compositional prompts to be handled within the same contact-grounded static HSI formulation.

\section{Scene-Aware Human Motion and 4D HSI}
\label{sec:scene-aware-motion-4d-hsi}

Dynamic human-scene interaction extends static body placement to motion over time. Instead of synthesizing one plausible configuration, a 4D HSI method must generate a temporally coherent sequence in which the body navigates through the environment, avoids obstacles, makes and breaks contacts, and satisfies the intended action. This introduces constraints that do not appear in single-frame synthesis: foot motion should not slide, trajectories should remain feasible, transitions should be smooth, contacts should be temporally consistent, and object interactions may involve changing object state. As a result, scene-aware motion synthesis has developed a distinct set of representations and evaluation criteria, including trajectory realism, collision avoidance, foot contact, temporal smoothness, long-horizon consistency, and semantic alignment with an action or text instruction.

Early scene-aware motion methods focused on generating long-term human behavior in 3D environments by combining motion priors with scene constraints. Wang et al. proposed a hierarchical framework for synthesizing long-term 3D human motion conditioned on scene structure, using geometry constraints between the human mesh and scene point clouds to improve physical plausibility \citep{wang2021longtermhsi}. Scene-aware generative models later factorized motion into trajectory generation and pose dynamics conditioned on the surrounding scene \citep{wang2022sceneaware}. These works helped establish that motion generation cannot be treated as scene-agnostic animation followed by placement: the environment determines where the body can move, where it can stand, and which objects can support or constrain an action.

Motion-primitive and policy-based methods further introduced controllability and long-horizon synthesis. SAMP models stochastic scene-aware motion prediction, allowing virtual humans to navigate complex scenes and perform goal-directed interactions with target objects \citep{hassan2021samp}. GAMMA generates long-term motion using generative motion primitives represented by body-surface markers, enabling diverse digital humans to move through 3D scenes while maintaining plausible body-ground contact \citep{zhang2022gamma}. DIMOS formulates human behavior synthesis in 3D indoor scenes as a policy-learning problem with latent motion action spaces, combining navigation, collision avoidance, and interaction goals to produce diverse motions such as wandering, sitting, lying, and standing up \citep{zhao2023dimos}. These methods are important because they connect scene-aware interaction to planning and control, but they still require learned motion priors and are primarily designed for whole-body motion over time rather than fine-grained static contact localization.

Diffusion models have become a dominant paradigm for scene-aware motion generation. SceneDiffuser formulates scene-conditioned generation, optimization, and planning as a unified diffusion process that can handle human pose and motion generation, dexterous grasp generation, path planning, and robot motion planning in 3D scenes \citep{wang2023scenediffuser}. This is significant because diffusion sampling can combine generative diversity with constraint-driven refinement. TeSMo uses denoising diffusion for text-controlled scene-aware motion generation, first learning a scene-agnostic text-to-motion model and then introducing scene-aware components for navigation and interaction \citep{yi2024tesmo}. HUMANISE and TextSceneMotion similarly use language to control human motion in 3D scenes, with TextSceneMotion explicitly decomposing the problem into target-object grounding and object-centric motion generation \citep{wang2022humanise,cen2024textscenemotion}. Together, these methods show that language, scene geometry, and motion priors can be integrated, but their output is a motion sequence rather than a single optimized contact state.

A key design question in dynamic HSI is how to represent the scene. Dense 3D representations such as point clouds, voxel grids, signed distance fields, and occupancy grids preserve geometric information but can be expensive and difficult to scale. Object-centric representations reduce scene complexity but may lose global layout context. Recent work explores hybrid or lightweight alternatives. SceMoS separates global planning from local physical execution by using a bird's-eye-view image with DINOv2 features for high-level scene reasoning and local 2D heightmaps for geometry-grounded motion tokenization \citep{ghosh2026scemos}. SceneMI represents scene-aware motion in-betweening with global scene descriptors and local body-centered Basis Point Set features, using diffusion to synthesize transitions that satisfy keyframe constraints while adapting to environmental affordances \citep{hwang2025scenemi}. These methods are relevant because they illustrate that dynamic HSI often requires both global layout understanding and local contact-aware geometry.

The scale of supervision is another major issue. Dynamic HSI benefits from paired motion-scene datasets because the model must learn not only plausible static contacts but also how humans approach, use, and leave objects over time. TRUMANS directly addresses this need by collecting a large motion-captured HSI dataset with diverse interactions across many indoor scene configurations and by training a diffusion-based autoregressive model for long, scene-conditioned interaction sequences \citep{jiang2024trumans}. The dataset and model demonstrate how performance improves when large-scale scene-motion supervision is available. At the same time, the need for such data highlights a limitation for open-vocabulary HSI: collecting exhaustive motion, object-state, and contact supervision for arbitrary scene functions remains difficult.

Zero-shot 4D HSI methods attempt to reduce this dependence by using video generation models as dynamic interaction priors. ZeroHSI distills human-scene interactions from video generation models, generating a video of a human interacting with a 3D scene and then reconstructing the 4D interaction through differentiable rendering \citep{li2026zerohsi}. Its motivation is closely related to zero-shot static HSI: pretrained visual models have observed a broad range of human interactions and can provide priors that are difficult to capture in paired 3D datasets. GenHSI also uses foundation video and image models, but decomposes controllable HSI video generation into script writing, 3D-aware keyframe generation, and animation \citep{li2026genhsi}. By generating keyframes that encode important interaction states before using video diffusion for temporal interpolation, GenHSI shows that static interaction poses can serve as anchors for dynamic HSI.

Despite these advances, dynamic HSI methods solve a different problem from this thesis. Their central challenge is to generate plausible motion through time: trajectories, transitions, foot contact, temporal smoothness, and long-horizon consistency. The thesis instead focuses on a static but more geometrically accountable target: a single SMPL-X body configuration that realizes an open-vocabulary interaction in a reconstructed ScanNet++ scene. This narrower output allows the method to emphasize body-region--scene-region contact correctness, collision avoidance, and metric alignment. In particular, it allows the scene-side contact region to be inferred from visual generative evidence and then enforced directly during SMPL-X optimization, rather than being treated as an implicit property of a motion sequence.

\section{Foundation Models for Zero-Shot HSI}
\label{sec:foundation-models-zero-shot-hsi}

Recent progress in foundation models has changed the design space for human-scene interaction synthesis. Instead of learning every interaction prior from paired 3D human-scene data, zero-shot methods use pretrained models as sources of semantic, visual, geometric, and motion knowledge. Large language models can decompose instructions and infer likely interaction structure; vision-language models can judge whether a rendered or generated image matches an action; image diffusion models can synthesize plausible human-scene compositions; video diffusion models can provide priors over temporal interaction; segmentation models can localize objects and regions in images; and monocular human reconstruction models can initialize parametric bodies. These models do not directly solve metric 3D HSI, but they provide hypotheses that can be grounded through camera calibration, scene geometry, and optimization.

Image diffusion models are particularly important because they encode broad visual priors over people, objects, and everyday interactions. Latent diffusion models showed that high-quality image synthesis and inpainting can be performed efficiently in a compressed latent space \citep{rombach2022ldm}. ControlNet further demonstrated that pretrained text-to-image diffusion models can be guided by spatial controls such as depth, edges, segmentation, and human pose \citep{zhang2023controlnet}. For HSI, this means that 2D generative models can be used not only to synthesize a person in a scene, but also to provide visual evidence about pose, orientation, object use, and plausible contact. However, such evidence is image-space and viewpoint-dependent. To be useful for 3D HSI, it must be converted into metric constraints in the reconstructed scene.

GenZI is a key example of this strategy in zero-shot static 3D HSI \citep{li2024genzi}. Given a 3D scene, a text prompt, and a coarse interaction location, GenZI renders the scene from multiple views and uses vision-language and image inpainting models to imagine plausible 2D human-scene interactions. It then optimizes a 3D human body to match these generated 2D hypotheses. This shows that 2D foundation models can provide interaction priors without training on paired 3D HSI data. The important limitation, from the perspective of this thesis, is that the generated image primarily acts as an implicit pose and appearance hypothesis. Fine-grained scene-side contact regions, such as a handle, ladder rung, treadmill frame, or drawer edge, are not treated as explicit optimization targets.

FunHSI moves zero-shot HSI closer to functional reasoning \citep{liu2026funhsi}. It addresses open-vocabulary task prompts by identifying functional scene elements, constructing a contact graph, synthesizing a human performing the task, and refining the 3D body through optimization. This is closely related to the motivation of this thesis because many interactions cannot be solved from object category alone. Instructions such as `increase the room temperature,'' `open the bottom drawer,'' or ``turn on the faucet'' require reasoning about object functionality and the body configuration needed to activate that functionality. FunHSI demonstrates the value of combining foundation-model reasoning with explicit contact structure. The present thesis follows this broad direction, but uses a different mechanism for scene-side grounding: it does not require assigning semantic names to object parts. Instead, the precise contact region is inferred visually as colored contact annotations on the scene image and then projected to the 3D mesh.

PhySIC is related from the reconstruction side rather than the synthesis side \citep{muralidhar2025physic}. It reconstructs physically plausible human-scene pairs and dense contact from a single RGB image by combining monocular cues with optimization over body pose, scene geometry, global scale, contact, and interpenetration. Although PhySIC assumes an observed human rather than generating a new human from an instruction, it is relevant because it illustrates the same principle that appears in zero-shot HSI: pretrained perceptual estimates are insufficient unless they are reconciled with physical constraints. In both reconstruction and synthesis, the final result must be evaluated in 3D, not only in the original image.

Foundation models have also influenced human-object interaction generation. HOI-Diff synthesizes text-driven 3D human-object interactions using diffusion models, including mechanisms for modeling contact between the human and object \citep{peng2023hoidiff}. InterDiff uses physics-informed diffusion to anticipate future human-object interactions with dynamic objects \citep{xu2023interdiff}. DreamHOI uses text-to-image diffusion priors for zero-shot 3D human-object interaction synthesis with a given object and textual description \citep{zhu2024dreamhoi}. InterFusion similarly uses text and diffusion-based visual priors for 3D human-object interaction generation \citep{dai2024interfusion}. HOI-PAGE is especially relevant because it introduces Part Affordance Graphs for zero-shot 4D HOI generation, using large language models to reason about how human body parts should engage with object parts before generating reference interaction videos and optimizing part-level contact constraints \citep{li2025hoipage}. These works show that diffusion and language priors can help bridge high-level descriptions and physical interaction. However, object-centric HOI differs from full-scene HSI: the target object is usually isolated or explicitly provided, whereas HSI requires reasoning about an object within a larger scene, resolving spatial context, floor support, occlusion, surrounding obstacles, and contact with the reconstructed environment.

Video foundation models extend these ideas to 4D HSI. ZeroHSI uses video generation models as dynamic interaction priors and reconstructs human-scene interaction motion through differentiable rendering \citep{li2026zerohsi}. This is significant because it treats pretrained video generation not merely as a visual output mechanism, but as a source of interaction knowledge learned from large-scale video data. GenHSI uses a training-free pipeline that decomposes HSI video generation into script writing, 3D-aware keyframe generation, and animation with a pretrained video diffusion model \citep{li2026genhsi}. In both cases, foundation models provide plausible temporal interaction hypotheses. For this thesis, these methods are relevant because they show that generated images or videos can serve as intermediate representations for 3D interaction synthesis. The difference is that this thesis uses a static image-based formulation to extract and enforce explicit contact regions rather than reconstructing full motion.

Segmentation foundation models are another important component for grounding visual hypotheses. Segment Anything introduced promptable image segmentation and showed strong zero-shot transfer to new image distributions and tasks \citep{kirillov2023sam}. SAM 3 extends this direction toward concept-prompted segmentation in images and videos, returning masks and instance identities for objects specified by noun phrases, exemplars, or combinations of prompts \citep{carion2025sam3}. Such models are useful in HSI because contact reasoning often requires separating the target object, floor, or support surface from the rest of the scene image. In this thesis, floor contact is treated as a special case: when the Scene Interaction Graph includes foot-floor contact, floor segmentation in the view is obtained and projected to the scene mesh. This differs from target-object contact, where the precise functional region is inferred from visual contact annotations rather than from a generic object mask.

Pretrained human mesh recovery models provide another form of foundation prior. Monocular human reconstruction methods can estimate a parametric body from an image, giving an initial pose, shape, and global configuration that can be refined in the scene. This thesis uses GVHMR to initialize the SMPL-X body from the inpainted human image \citep{shen2024gvhmr}. GVHMR is useful because it predicts a world-grounded human configuration rather than only an image-aligned body estimate. Nevertheless, the recovered pose is still only an initialization. It may not satisfy the intended contact relation or avoid all scene collisions. Therefore, the pose must be refined using contact, penetration, pose-prior, and orientation-prior losses.

The proposed pipeline combines these foundation-model roles into a zero-shot static HSI system. A language model converts the user instruction into a Scene Interaction Graph, whose nodes are human body regions and the target object or floor, and whose edges specify required contacts. A detailed prompt is used to inpaint a plausible human into the ScanNet++ scene image. GVHMR estimates an initial SMPL-X body from this inpainted human image. The inpainted human image and original scene image are then used to ask a generative image model to mark each required scene-side contact region with a distinct bright color, for example using different colors for left-hand and right-hand contacts. These colored overlays are converted into binary masks and projected back to the reconstructed scene mesh. Finally, SMPL-X optimization enforces the projected contact targets while penalizing interpenetration and keeping the optimized body close to the initialization.

The common limitation of foundation-model-based HSI is that foundation models operate primarily in language, image, or video space. They can propose plausible interactions, infer likely body-object relations, or hallucinate visually convincing contacts, but they do not automatically provide metrically valid 3D constraints. For a reconstructed-scene output, their predictions must be grounded in camera geometry, scene meshes, and physically meaningful optimization objectives. This thesis addresses that conversion explicitly: foundation models provide semantic and visual interaction priors, while projection and SMPL-X optimization convert those priors into contact targets and a final body configuration in the reconstructed 3D scene.

\section{Image Diffusion, Segmentation, and 2D-to-3D Contact Localization}
\label{sec:image-diffusion-contact-localization}

Image-space foundation models provide a powerful source of visual interaction priors for human-scene interaction synthesis. Text-to-image and image-editing diffusion models have learned broad regularities about how people appear in scenes, how bodies are posed relative to objects, and which visual configurations are plausible for everyday actions. In HSI, these priors are useful because many interactions are easier to imagine in an image than to specify directly as 3D constraints. However, image generation alone is insufficient for 3D synthesis. A generated person may look plausible from the input view while still being geometrically inconsistent with the reconstructed scene. The central challenge is therefore to convert image-space visual evidence into 3D constraints that can guide body optimization.

Diffusion-based inpainting and editing provide the basis for using generated images as interaction hypotheses. Latent diffusion models enable high-quality image synthesis and inpainting in a compressed latent space \citep{rombach2022ldm}, while methods such as RePaint and InstructPix2Pix demonstrate the effectiveness of diffusion models for image completion and instruction-guided editing \citep{lugmayr2022repaint,brooks2023instructpix2pix}. ControlNet further shows that pretrained image diffusion models can be guided by spatial conditions such as depth, edges, segmentation, and pose \citep{zhang2023controlnet}. More recent instruction-following image generation systems, including GPT-Image and Nano Banana 2, extend this capability with stronger multimodal prompting and image editing behavior \citep{openai2025gptimage,google2026nanobanana2}. These models make it possible to ask for a human to be inserted into a given scene image according to a natural language instruction, producing a visually plausible interaction hypothesis without training a task-specific HSI model.

Several HSI and HOI methods already use 2D generative priors as bridges to 3D interaction. GenZI renders a 3D scene into image views, uses image inpainting and vision-language reasoning to synthesize plausible 2D human-scene interactions, and then lifts these hypotheses to a 3D human body through optimization \citep{li2024genzi}. GenHSI similarly uses image generation to create 3D-aware keyframes before video diffusion produces continuous HSI videos \citep{li2026genhsi}. DreamHOI and related zero-shot HOI methods use text-to-image diffusion priors to synthesize human-object interaction cues for 3D interaction generation \citep{zhu2024dreamhoi}. HOI-PAGE is especially relevant because it uses part-level affordance reasoning to guide zero-shot 4D human-object interaction generation, connecting human body parts and object parts before generating interaction videos and optimizing contacts \citep{li2025hoipage}. These works show that visual generative models can provide interaction hypotheses beyond what is available in paired 3D datasets.

The limitation is that most generated visual hypotheses remain implicit. A generated image may show a hand near a handle, a body sitting on a chair, or a foot on a ladder rung, but the image itself does not directly define which scene mesh vertices should be contacted by which SMPL-X body region. For metric HSI, this distinction is important. The output must not only look like an interaction from the camera view; it must also satisfy contact and collision constraints in 3D. Thus, a useful image prior should ideally be converted into explicit scene-side contact targets rather than used only as a qualitative visual guide.

Segmentation and visual localization models provide one way to convert image evidence into spatial constraints. Segment Anything introduced promptable image segmentation for zero-shot mask extraction \citep{kirillov2023sam}. SAM 3 extends this direction with concept-prompted segmentation in images and videos, making it possible to segment regions specified by semantic prompts \citep{carion2025sam3}. In this thesis, segmentation is used in a targeted way rather than as a general object grounding pipeline. Floor contact is treated as a special support case: when the Scene Interaction Graph contains foot-floor edges, SAM 3 is used to obtain a floor mask in the input view, which can then be projected to the scene mesh. This is appropriate because the exact foot location on the floor is often flexible, provided that the feet remain supported and physically plausible.

Target-object contacts require a different treatment. For many interactions, the contact region is not the whole object mask and may not have a stable semantic label. A hand may need to contact a treadmill side frame, a faucet control, a bike handle, a ladder rung, a drawer front, or an edge of an object. Instead of first detecting and naming such object parts, this thesis uses the interaction image itself as evidence for where contact should occur. After a human is inpainted into the ScanNet++ view according to the detailed interaction prompt, the inpainted human image and the original scene image are given to an image generation model together with a Scene Interaction Graph-based instruction. The model is asked to mark each required scene-side contact region on the original scene image using a distinct bright color, such as red for one hand contact and blue for another. The colored overlays are then converted into binary masks.

This formulation differs from standard segmentation and image editing. The goal is not to segment the object category, edit the image for visual realism, or produce a final 2D result. Instead, the generated colored annotations act as an intermediate contact representation. Because the marks are produced on the original scene image without the inpainted human, they can be associated with visible scene regions rather than with the generated body appearance. These 2D masks are then projected back to the reconstructed ScanNet++ mesh using the calibrated camera and scene geometry. The projected regions become explicit scene-side contact targets for SMPL-X optimization.

This 2D-to-3D conversion is the key link between foundation-model reasoning and metric HSI. Image generation provides a visual prior about where the interaction should occur; segmentation provides support-region masks when the target is geometrically broad, such as the floor; projection transfers the image-space evidence to the scene mesh; and optimization enforces the resulting contact constraints on the SMPL-X body. This allows the method to use flexible open-vocabulary image models while retaining a 3D output that is accountable to contact, penetration, pose prior, and orientation prior losses.

The main gap addressed by this thesis is therefore not simply the use of image diffusion for HSI, but the use of image diffusion for contact localization. Prior work uses diffusion and segmentation primarily to generate plausible humans, edit images, create interaction videos, or segment objects. In contrast, this thesis uses image generation to annotate scene-side contact regions conditioned on body-part contact requirements. The generated colored overlays are not the final output; they are converted into 3D contact targets and used to optimize a static SMPL-X body in the reconstructed scene.

\section{Geometry-Aware Optimization for Physical Plausibility}
\label{sec:geometry-aware-optimization}

Geometry-aware optimization is a recurring component in human-scene interaction because generative or monocular predictions alone rarely satisfy metric scene constraints. A predicted or generated body may be visually plausible from a camera view but physically invalid in 3D, for example by floating above the floor, penetrating furniture, missing the intended contact surface, or rotating away from the target object. Optimization provides a way to correct these errors by refining a parametric body model with respect to the reconstructed scene. In this thesis, optimization is especially important because foundation models provide image-space hypotheses, while the final output must be a physically plausible SMPL-X body in the ScanNet++ scene coordinate frame.

The optimization tradition in human body recovery begins with fitting parametric body models to observations under pose and shape priors. SMPLify fits SMPL to 2D keypoints using an optimization objective that combines reprojection consistency with body priors and interpenetration penalties \citep{bogo2016smplify}. SMPLify-X extends this idea to SMPL-X, enabling joint fitting of body, hands, and face from monocular images \citep{pavlakos2019smplx}. These methods show the value of optimizing a differentiable body model rather than directly regressing a mesh. However, image-only fitting is underconstrained: many 3D body configurations can explain the same 2D evidence, and without the surrounding scene the recovered body can violate physical constraints.

PROX addresses this limitation by adding scene constraints to monocular SMPL-X fitting \citep{hassan2019prox}. It introduces an interpenetration term that penalizes the body for occupying the same space as the reconstructed environment and a contact term that encourages plausible body regions to touch nearby scene surfaces. This is a crucial step for HSI because it treats the scene as part of the optimization problem rather than as a background for rendering. PROX shows that contact and collision constraints can reduce pose ambiguity and produce bodies that are more consistent with the physical world. Later HSI synthesis methods inherit this idea by using scene geometry not only to evaluate results but also to refine them.

PLACE and POSA further connect optimization with learned contact priors. PLACE uses local scene geometry and proximity-aware representations to generate bodies that are compatible with 3D environments, followed by refinement for physical plausibility \citep{zhang2020place}. POSA predicts body-surface contacts and scene semantics on SMPL-X, providing a learned prior for where contact should occur between the body and scene \citep{hassan2021posa}. These methods demonstrate that optimization benefits from good contact proposals: the optimizer needs targets or priors that indicate which body regions should approach which scene surfaces. Without such guidance, contact losses can be ambiguous, especially in cluttered scenes where many surfaces are nearby but only one is functionally relevant.

Zero-shot HSI methods also rely on optimization to convert foundation-model hypotheses into 3D bodies. GenZI uses generated 2D interaction hypotheses from rendered views and optimizes a 3D human body to match them in the scene \citep{li2024genzi}. FunHSI refines proposed 3D body and hand poses through a stage-wise optimization process to improve physical plausibility and functional correctness \citep{liu2026funhsi}. PhySIC, although focused on reconstruction rather than synthesis, similarly combines monocular perceptual estimates with physical optimization over body pose, scene geometry, contact, interpenetration, and scale \citep{muralidhar2025physic}. These works show a common pattern: foundation or perception models provide initialization and semantic cues, while optimization enforces geometric consistency.

Efficient collision and penetration modeling is an important part of such optimization. Traditional mesh-based collision handling can be expensive or unstable when many body and scene points must be tested. Recent neural volumetric body models provide more efficient alternatives. VolumetricSMPL extends SMPL-family models with volumetric signed-distance functionality, enabling fast and differentiable SDF queries for interactions, contacts, and collision detection \citep{mihajlovic2025volumetricsmpl}. This is useful for HSI optimization because scene points can be queried against the posed body volume to penalize points that lie inside the body. In this thesis, penetration is handled by sampling scene points and evaluating them with the SMPL-X SDF provided by VolumetricSMPL, allowing the optimization to discourage scene-body intersections while remaining compatible with the SMPL-X body parameterization.

The proposed optimization follows this general lineage but uses a different source of contact targets. The initial body is obtained from GVHMR applied to the inpainted human image, providing SMPL-X translation, rotation, and pose parameters \citep{shen2024gvhmr}. The optimization then refines only the body translation, global rotation, and pose. Shape, camera parameters, and scene geometry are kept fixed. Contact is enforced by minimizing distances between selected body-side contact vertices and projected scene-side contact vertices. Penetration is penalized using the VolumetricSMPL SDF. Pose and orientation priors are implemented as penalties that keep the optimized SMPL-X parameters close to the GVHMR initialization, preventing contact objectives from producing unnatural deformations or implausible rotations.

The key difference from prior optimization-based HSI is the origin and granularity of the contact constraints. Previous methods often use learned contact priors, observed human contacts, nearby surfaces, or object-level interaction labels. In contrast, this thesis uses diffusion-derived scene-side contact regions as explicit optimization targets. The contact regions are inferred in image space from the original scene image, the inpainted human image, and the Scene Interaction Graph, then projected to the reconstructed mesh. Optimization therefore acts as the final grounding step: it converts visually inferred contact evidence into a physically plausible SMPL-X configuration while preserving the monocular initialization and avoiding scene penetration.

\section{Summary and Research Gap}
\label{sec:related-work-gap}

Prior work on human-scene interaction has established several important foundations for this thesis. Static 3D HSI methods show that plausible human placement requires contact-aware body-scene compatibility, semantic control, and collision avoidance \citep{li2019putting,zhang2020generating,zhang2020place,hassan2021posa,zhao2022coins}. Dynamic and 4D HSI methods extend this setting to trajectories, long-horizon motion, temporal contact consistency, and text-conditioned scene-aware motion \citep{wang2022humanise,wang2023scenediffuser,zhao2023dimos,cen2024textscenemotion,yi2024tesmo,jiang2024trumans,ghosh2026scemos}. These works demonstrate that HSI can be modeled effectively when sufficient supervision, scene representations, and interaction priors are available. However, many of them depend on paired human-scene or human-object interaction data, predefined action spaces, object-level semantic labels, or motion datasets whose coverage is necessarily limited relative to open-vocabulary everyday interactions.

Zero-shot and foundation-model-based methods reduce this dependence by using pretrained language, vision-language, image generation, video generation, segmentation, and monocular reconstruction models as sources of interaction knowledge. GenZI shows that 2D visual priors can be distilled into 3D HSI without paired 3D interaction training data \citep{li2024genzi}. FunHSI extends this direction toward open-vocabulary functional interaction by reasoning about functional scene elements and contact structure \citep{liu2026funhsi}. ZeroHSI and GenHSI show that video and image generation models can serve as priors for dynamic HSI and interaction keyframes \citep{li2026zerohsi,li2026genhsi}. Object-centric HOI methods, including HOI-Diff, DreamHOI, InterFusion, and HOI-PAGE, further show that diffusion and language priors can support contact-aware human-object interaction generation, with HOI-PAGE being especially relevant for its part-level affordance reasoning \citep{peng2023hoidiff,zhu2024dreamhoi,dai2024interfusion,li2025hoipage}. Nevertheless, these methods often focus on visual plausibility, object-level interaction, or motion generation rather than precise static body-region--scene-region contact grounding in a reconstructed full scene.

The key research gap addressed in this thesis is fine-grained functional contact grounding for zero-shot static 3D HSI. Open-vocabulary instructions frequently require contact with a specific scene subregion rather than with an entire object instance. A person turning on a faucet, holding a treadmill side frame, climbing a ladder, taking a bike off a stand, or sitting on a sofa while interacting with a nearby object must place the correct body region on the correct functional scene region. Existing approaches provide important ingredients: learned contact priors, action-object semantic control, language-conditioned motion generation, foundation-model visual priors, and optimization-based physical refinement. What remains underexplored is the direct conversion of generated visual interaction evidence into explicit 3D contact targets for body optimization.

This thesis introduces a zero-shot static HSI formulation for ScanNet++ scenes that combines open-vocabulary reasoning with diffusion-based fine-grained contact localization. Given a scene image, camera parameters, and reconstructed scene mesh, the method first uses language reasoning to determine which body regions should contact the target object or floor. An image generation model then inpaints a plausible human performing the requested interaction. The central novelty is that image generation is also used to produce colored contact annotations on the original scene image: each required contact region is marked visually according to the body-region contact requirements. These colored annotations are converted into masks, projected onto the reconstructed mesh, and used as explicit scene-side contact targets.

The final SMPL-X body is obtained by optimizing the GVHMR initialization in the reconstructed scene coordinate frame using contact, penetration, pose-prior, and orientation-prior losses. This connects foundation-model flexibility with metric geometric accountability. In contrast to methods that rely on closed interaction datasets or use generated images only as implicit visual hypotheses, the proposed approach treats diffusion-generated contact evidence as an intermediate representation for 3D optimization. In this way, the thesis builds on prior work in contact-aware HSI, language-conditioned interaction, zero-shot visual priors, and geometry-aware refinement, while focusing specifically on the missing problem of fine-grained functional contact grounding in static 3D human-scene interaction.
